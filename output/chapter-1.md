# Chapter 1

*Genre: epic fantasy | Chapter Brief: She's still in the memory mines — underground, exhausted, hauling crystal crates. A crystal breaks. She accidentally absorbs it. She discovers what she is. She steals a crystal and escapes using the dead mason's skill. She surfaces into ash-fall for the first time.*

---

She had carried crates since before she knew what carrying was.

The sorting lines ran six days in seven. Up at the fourth bell, ash-cloth wrapping, load the crates, walk the line, sort, don't look at the crystals too long. *Don't drop anything.* The Harvesters came at shift-end with their tallies, and as long as your number was right, you went back to your bunk. She had been in the mines since she was four years old, or perhaps five — the records were kept by people who did not share them — and the rhythm of this was simply the rhythm of her body, the way her heartbeat was a rhythm, the way her breath was.

She carried the crates with her knees bent and her back straight. The crystal inside was wrapped in ash-cloth. She could feel the weight shift when she moved too fast.

The thing nobody said aloud — not because it was secret, but because it was too obvious to say — was that the system worked perfectly. Not for the workers. For the system. The Harvesters did not need to watch everyone. The workers watched each other. The foreman did not need to punish most infractions. The possibility of punishment was enough. The crystals did not need to glow with the memories of dead people in a threatening way. They just glowed. The threat came from somewhere above the crystals, in a chain of authority that ascended until it reached a god who had been killing people for a thousand years, and you did not need to see that god to feel his weight at the bottom of every shaft.

She had been feeling that weight since she was four.

The crystals were the memories of the dead. She had always known this in the way one knows water is cold or stone is heavy — without ever having thought it worth thinking about. The dead were sorted and wrapped and shipped to collectors who served something she did not name. In the mines, no one named it.

The woman named Netta, three paces ahead of her in the line, dropped her crate.

The sound of a crystal breaking is unlike any other sound. It does not shatter. It sings, briefly, in a register almost beyond hearing — and then it goes silent in a way that fills the ears like grief. The light inside it dispersed across the tunnel floor in a color that had no name in any language spoken in the mines.

Everyone stopped. Everyone looked at the floor.

The foreman came from the sorting hall. He did not run. He walked the way men walked when they knew they were in charge of what happened next. Two Harvesters came through the gate.

*The Harvesters were efficient about it. That was the worst thing about the Harvesters.*

She set her crate down and braced her hand flat against the floor — she had developed this reflex over years, the habit of finding a surface whenever anything alarming happened, on the theory that it was difficult to make things worse by touching the floor.

The broken crystal's light found her palm.

What followed was not like remembering. It was more like being translated into a language she had never learned, and discovering that the language contained concepts her own lacked — and that some of those concepts fit something she had felt for years without having the word for it. It was like standing outside on a cold night and someone opening a door behind you, and for one second feeling the warmth of the whole room on your back, except the room was a man's entire life.

He was a mason of the third order, and his name was Aldric Vann, and he had set the capstone of the bridge at the river Selt; who had kissed his wife Betha on a cold morning that smelled of bread and river water and not known it was the last; who had carried a piece of her bread for half a day before it crumbled and had been ashamed of this, afterward, though there was no one to be ashamed in front of; who had a bad left knee and two sons and forty-three years of knowing exactly what stone wanted to do and how to help it or stop it. He had died alone on a road in winter and the road had been named for someone else.

She knew all of this. She knew the name of the song he hummed when the work was going well. She knew which type of cold meant a hollow behind stone. She knew that the north wall of tunnel six — which she had walked past ten thousand times without seeing it the way it wished to be seen — had been bearing load it was never built to carry, had been lying about this for sixteen years, and would release with one deliberate push at precisely the correct point.

She knew his whole life. It was complete and it was hers.

And then the door swung shut.

She pulled her hand back. She pressed it against her thigh.

Nobody had seen.

The Harvesters were taking Netta through the gate. The other workers had their eyes down. The foreman was writing in his ledger.

She looked at her hands.

The skin across her knuckles was different. Loose. Thin. Paper-white over the architecture of bone in a way it had not been four seconds ago. She pressed her thumb against the back of her hand and watched the skin move slowly back, the way old skin moves. She turned her hands over. The lines in her palms were deeper.

*Something was given and something was taken, and the giving was not free.*

She did not understand what this meant. She filed it the way she filed everything: labeled it, set it aside, moved on. She picked up her crate and walked to the sorting station. The clerk did not look up. She deposited the crate. She walked back.

She thought about tunnel six.

At the reserve bins she took one crystal without breaking stride — small, pale green, the size of two fingers. She pressed it flat against her ribs, under her shirt. Old Corvus in sorting position three was sleeping standing up. The foreman was writing.

In tunnel six she walked to the north wall. Third bracket. She pressed her palm flat against the stone at the place where only a mason of forty-three years would have thought to press it, and she felt the lie of it — the particular cold of a wall pretending to hold more than it could, the subtle wrongness in the weight.

She pushed.

The stone panel came out of the wall cleanly, the way a truth comes out of someone who has been holding it too long — all at once, and with a kind of relief. The wall, which had been building up to this for sixteen years, was somewhat relieved about it.

Behind it was darkness and moving air. She had never felt moving air underground before.

She went through.

She went upward through the natural passage for a long time, using her hands on the walls, using Aldric's knowledge of how rock felt when it was safe to trust — and feeling that knowledge thin and drift at the edges with each passing minute, the way you forget a dream you don't write down. When she finally emerged onto a hillside, she did not know much more about stone than she had that morning.

She had known enough to get here.

---

She stood in the open for the first time she could remember and could not, for a long moment, let go of the stone lip of the passage behind her.

The sky was enormous. She had known intellectually that it was enormous. She had not known that *enormous*, in this context, would do the same work that *wet* does in the phrase *the ocean is wet* — a word that turns out to mean something categorically different when you are standing inside the thing it names.

The sky was pale, the color of old bone. Something was falling from it. Small and slow, in spirals, with no urgency — the way things fall when they are not afraid of landing.

She held out her hand. The falling thing landed on her palm, cold and soft and specifically itself. It dissolved.

She held out her hand again. Same.

She stood with her arms out, catching what fell and letting it go, catching it and letting it go. She had heard women in the mines talk about water that fell from the sky. She had not known it would feel like this. Quiet. Patient. Like the world dissolving into her, one small cold piece at a time.

In her pocket: one intact crystal, pressing against her ribs. In her hands: the ghost of a dead man's knowledge, already forgetting. Behind her: a broken wall that the Harvesters would find before nightfall. Before her: everything she had no name for yet.

You are the gap in an inheritance, she thought, or perhaps only felt — words that arrived from no particular source, that were true in a way she couldn't explain. You are the hole where a history should be.

She had never been above ground. She did not know what rain was called. She did not know which direction was safe, or whether any direction was safe, or what safe meant out here in the world's enormous openness.

She let go of the stone.

She started walking — knees bent, back straight, the way she had always walked, the way the mines had shaped her, and which she carried with her into a world that had not yet told her what shape it needed her to be.

The ash fell. The horizon did not get closer.

She walked toward it anyway.

**From King:** The rhythm and italics of *Don't drop anything*; the analogy of the warm door on a cold night; the genuine accessible emotional register.

**From Austen:** The devastating compression of returning to herself.

**From Abercrombie:** The grimdark system analysis in the opening paragraphs; the Harvesters being efficient — "that was the worst thing."
