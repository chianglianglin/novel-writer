# Agatha Christie Draft

**Confidence:** 77%

**Style Notes:** Christie's observational precision applied to the social ecology of the mines; the protagonist reads people as data; the absorption delivered in Christie's matter-of-fact revelatory style; emphasis on what was noticed and what wasn't; the closing moves from assembly of facts to forward momentum.

The sorting hall of the seventh shaft ran to a schedule that permitted no deviation. At the second bell, the first line loaded. At the third bell, the second line loaded. At the fourth bell, the supervisory count began, and any discrepancy in the crystal tally was reported upward to the Harvesters by the end of the shift.

This was the system. Everyone in the mines knew it. The system's genius was that it punished deviation from below without requiring active cruelty from above. The Harvesters did not need to watch constantly. The workers watched one another.

She had understood this since childhood. She had also understood, from careful observation over eight years, exactly which workers watched and which ones only appeared to watch.

The woman named Reth, for instance, kept her eyes down when the foreman passed and raised them again the moment he turned. Old Corvus in sorting position three had the look of a man attending to his crate when in fact he was sleeping standing up — she had confirmed this twice by dropping something within his eyeline. And the foreman himself had a habit of writing in his ledger at the moments that mattered, as if paperwork were a shield against what was about to become his problem.

She had filed all of this away without knowing why it might be useful. Today it became useful.

It was Reth who dropped her crate.

The crack of the crystal was not a loud sound. It was precise — the sound of a thing engineered to contain enormous pressure suddenly ceasing to do so. The light from inside it shifted, pulsed once, and went out in a particular way that she had only seen twice and that she had observed, both times, preceded a removal from the workforce.

The tunnel went still. The foreman looked up from his ledger. Reth stood over the broken crystal with the expression of someone who has just understood a joke they will never be able to repeat.

She set her crate down carefully, in the correct posture, at the correct distance from the wall. She braced her hand against the floor.

The light from the broken crystal reached her skin.

It was not, she realized afterward, as dramatic as it should have been. She did not fall. She did not cry out. For approximately four seconds she was, in addition to being herself, also a man of middle years named Aldric, who knew how stone talked to itself in the dark — how a wall communicated its weakness to anyone trained to listen, through the particular cold it gave off, through the sound of a hand placed flat against it. She knew his wife. She knew the smell of the bread she made on cold mornings. She knew the name of the bridge he had built and the name he had given the keystone before he set it, which was the name of his eldest son.

Then she was only herself again. Her hand was on the floor. The Harvesters were coming through the gate. Reth was still standing over the broken crystal.

Nobody had looked at her. She had verified this in the first half-second after the absorption ended, which was interesting. She had not known she was the sort of person who verified things in the first half-second after extraordinary experiences. Apparently she was.

She rose carefully, collected her crate, and walked to the sorting station.

Her hands, she noticed, looked different. The skin across her knuckles was thinner — she could see the architecture of the bones more clearly than she could an hour ago. This struck her as a clue, the sort one filed and returned to.

She returned her crate and collected another. This one she would not deliver to the sorting hall. She left it at the end of the tunnel and visited the reserve bins, where she pocketed one small intact crystal without difficulty. Old Corvus was asleep in position three. The foreman was writing.

The north wall of tunnel six was the matter of particular interest. She had passed it so often that its unremarkability had become, in retrospect, suspicious. She pressed her palm against the stone at waist height, three feet left of the bracket, and felt what Aldric had spent years learning to feel: the sound a wall made when it was lying about how much weight it could hold.

She pushed.

The stone panel came out cleanly. Behind it was a passage and the smell of something cold and moving.

She went through.

She did not look back. She had found, in observing the workers who were taken by the Harvesters, that looking back was rarely productive.

She emerged on a hillside. The sky was pale, the color of old cloth, and something was falling from it in slow spirals. She held out her hand and caught one. It dissolved. She caught another. Same result.

She stood still for a moment and assembled what she knew. One intact crystal. Hands that had aged approximately ten years in four seconds, for reasons that wanted investigating. A broken wall that would be found by nightfall. No food. No map. No name for the things falling from the sky.

She had worked with less.

She began to walk, and as she walked she began to think carefully about what, exactly, had happened to her — and whether it was the sort of thing that could be made useful.
