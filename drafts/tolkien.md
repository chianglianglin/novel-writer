In the deepest age of Morthael's unending reign, the passages beneath the Ashfeld had been called by another name, in a tongue now mostly lost. The women who first worked the shafts had sung as they sorted — so it was said, though none living could verify it, and the Harvesters discouraged speech save what the work required.

She had been brought to the mines when she was small enough that a Harvester carried her in. She remembered nothing of before. She knew only the weight of crates, the smell of rock dust, the blue-white glow of crystals being loaded onto sleds bound for the upper levels where the collectors waited with their tallies and their careful gloves.

The crystals were the memories of the dead. She had always known this in the way one knows water is cold or stone is heavy — without having once thought it worth thinking about. The dead were everywhere in the mines, sealed in glass, waiting to be carried somewhere useful.

It was the woman named Elda, three places ahead of her in the line, who let her crate fall.

The sound of a crystal breaking is unlike any other sound. It does not shatter as glass shatters. It sings, briefly, in a register almost beyond hearing — and then it goes silent in a manner that fills the ears like grief.

Everyone in the tunnel stopped. The foreman came from the sorting hall. The Harvesters moved from the gate.

She set her crate down and braced her palm against the floor. The broken crystal's light reached her hand. What followed was not memory. It was more as if a door fell suddenly open in a wall she had never known was there, admitting all at once the light and weather and weight of a whole life she had never entered.

She was Aldric, mason of the third order, who had set the capstone of the bridge at the river Selt; who had kissed his wife on a cold morning that smelled of bread and river water and not known it was the last; who had died frightened and alone on a road later named for someone else. She knew the way fracture moved through limestone. She knew where a wall bore weight it was not built to carry. She knew forty-three years of sunlight and cold and sorrow and honest labour. It was all hers, all complete, and then the door swung shut.

She pulled her hand back. She sat still for a moment, which she had never done in the middle of a shift, and nobody noticed because the Harvesters were occupied with Elda.

She looked at her hands. The skin across her knuckles had grown thin — she could see the roads of blue vein beneath more clearly than she had before. She turned them over. She had not noticed, until now, how old they looked.

She thought about the north wall of tunnel six.

She had walked past it ten thousand times. She had never seen it as Aldric saw walls — as argument between stone and weight, as geometry solving itself over years. She looked at it now through his knowledge, and she saw that the stone three feet to the left of the third bracket was bearing load it had never been meant to bear. The keystone above it was cracked through. One deliberate pressure in the correct place would free the whole panel.

She set down her crate at the sorting station without breaking stride. She took one crystal from the reserve bin — small and the color of green leaves, though she had never seen leaves and the image came from somewhere borrowed. She pressed it to her ribs beneath her shirt.

In tunnel six she found the place on the wall and set her palms against it. She pushed.

The stone panel came free as though it had been held in place only by years of forgetting. Behind it, the world breathed. She could feel it — a movement of air from somewhere high above.

She climbed the natural passage with her hands and feet and Aldric's understanding of how rock holds weight. She did not know how long she climbed.

She came up onto a hillside under an open sky.

She had been told — in the fragments that passed between workers in the dark, cautious and half-believed — that the world above was vast. This was true. She stood at the top of the slope and looked out at a horizon she could not reach with her eyes. The sky was pale, the color of ash and winter. Something was falling from it — slowly, in spirals, touching her skin and dissolving without heat.

She did not have a name for this. She thought it might be what the women called snow, or perhaps the ash that she had always imagined was the only thing that fell. Either way, it was cold, and quiet, and it fell on her hands — her old-looking, borrowed hands — and melted without any sound at all.

The Harvesters would find the broken wall by nightfall.

She went forward into the world she did not know, carrying one crystal against her ribs and the ghost of a dead man's knowledge in her hands, which were already forgetting.
