# N.K. Jemisin Draft

**Confidence:** 86%

**Style Notes:** Second-person present tense sustained throughout; multiple layers woven without apology; magic grounded in body and cost (aging as payment); systemic oppression structural not decorative; closing revelation recontextualizes entire chapter — her escape was anticipated, she is running toward a war.

---

You have carried crates since before you knew what carrying was.

Here is something you know: the crystals are made from dead people. Here is something you have chosen not to think about: you are made from dead people too, in a sense — made from everything they did not pass down to you, every language and skill and face and name the Harvesters took before it could reach you. You are the gap in an inheritance. You are the hole where a history should be.

You are sixteen. The Harvesters have not told you this. You counted it yourself from the mark you made each year on the underside of your bunk.

Right now you are walking the fourth tunnel with a crate in your arms and the second bell in your ears. The crystal inside is wrapped in ash-cloth. You can feel it through the cloth — that particular cold, the way they're always slightly colder than they should be, like they're pulling heat from the air to replace something they lost.

The woman ahead of you — her name is Netta, you know her the way you know all the workers, which is carefully and from a distance — drops her crate.

*Stop.*

This is the moment. You will think about this moment for the rest of your life, or what passes for a life in the years that follow. You will think: if I had been one step back. If I had been looking at the ceiling. If my hand had been in my pocket.

But you are you, and you have survived twelve years in the mines by bracing yourself against any surface available when anything unexpected happens, and your hand is on the floor when the broken crystal's light finds it.

You are someone else.

You are Aldric Vann, and you are forty-three years old, and you are alive. You are building the bridge at the Selt crossing. You are touching your wife's hand at the kitchen table in the dark of an early morning. You are looking at a wall — this wall, or a wall like it, or perhaps all walls are the same wall when you know them the way Aldric knew them — and you are seeing the lie inside it. The weight that stone was never meant to carry. The place where one push would free everything.

You are yourself again.

Four seconds. That is all.

You notice: your hands. You have looked at your hands every day for twelve years. You know them. The skin across your knuckles is different now — loose, thin, papery in a way it was not four seconds ago. You are aging. You are paying for what you received, or what received itself into you.

You do not understand the mechanism. You will understand it later, after much pain. For now you only know: something was given and something was taken, and the giving was not free.

You stand up. You collect your crate.

(You are aware: somewhere above you, a thousand years ago, a god decided that the memories of the dead were currency. You are aware: the mines exist because of this decision. You are aware: the woman called Netta is currently being walked through the gate by two Harvesters who will not bring her back. You are aware of all of this. You have always been aware of all of this.)

(What is new is that you can do something about it.)

You deposit your crate at the sorting station. You take one crystal from the reserve bins — small, green, cold. You press it against your ribs.

You go to tunnel six.

The north wall. Third bracket. The place where Aldric Vann would have stopped and put his hand and felt the lie.

You put your hand there. You feel the lie.

You push.

The panel comes free. Behind it is air that moves.

You climb through the dark, through a natural passage that was never cut by any human hand, only by the slow intention of water and time. Your knees scrape stone. Your hands find holds that feel like they were always going to be there, waiting for you.

You surface on a hillside.

The sky is enormous. This is the first true thing you have learned about the outside: the sky is enormous and it has no ceiling and something is falling from it, small and soft and cold, and when it touches your skin it becomes water and disappears.

You stand with your arms out. You catch it. You let it go.

You have no map. You have no food. You have one crystal in your pocket and hands that are older than they should be and a wall broken behind you that the Harvesters will find before nightfall.

You are sixteen years old and you have never been above ground and you do not know what the falling things are called.

You start walking.

Here is what you do not know yet, standing on the hillside in the falling ash: the god-king already knows what you are. Has known, perhaps, since before you knew it yourself. The mines are not simply a place of extraction. They are a testing ground. They have been watching for you — or someone like you — for a very long time.

You are not running toward freedom.

You are running toward a war that began before you were born.
