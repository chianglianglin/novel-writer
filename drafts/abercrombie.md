The thing nobody in the mines told you — not because it was a secret, but because it was too obvious to bother saying — was that the system worked perfectly.

Not for the workers. Obviously not for the workers. For the system.

The Harvesters didn't need to watch everyone. The workers watched each other. The foremen didn't need to punish most infractions. The possibility of punishment was enough. The crystals didn't need to glow with the memories of dead people in a threatening way. They just glowed. The threat came from somewhere above the crystals, in a chain of authority that ascended upward until it reached a god who had been killing people for a thousand years, and you didn't need to see that god to feel the weight of him at the bottom of the shaft.

She had been feeling that weight since she was four years old.

The woman in front of her dropped her crate. The crystal inside broke with a sound that she would have described as a *crack* if asked, which she would not be, because no one in the mines asked anyone to describe anything.

Everyone stopped. Everyone looked at the floor. The foreman came. The Harvesters came. This was how it worked.

She was already kneeling — whether from instinct or the sort of luck that visits people occasionally and means nothing — with her hand against the floor.

The broken crystal's light hit her palm.

And then — this was the part that would take her years to explain to anyone, and which she never managed satisfactorily — she knew how to build a bridge.

Not only that. She knew the smell of bread baking in a stone oven on a cold morning. She knew the weight of a sleeping child carried up a flight of stairs. She knew forty-three years of a man named Aldric who had been a stonemason and a father and a husband and had died frightened in winter, on a road, in the dark. All of it arrived in her, absolute and complete and detailed down to the particular quality of regret he had felt at the end, and then it was gone.

Four seconds.

She was herself again, which was considerably less interesting.

She stood up. She collected her crate. Her hands looked older. She noted this the way she noted everything in the mines — filed it, labeled it *problem for later*, moved on.

The Harvesters were taking the other woman through the gate. They were efficient about it. That was the worst thing about the Harvesters, she had always thought. They were efficient about it.

She carried her crate to the sorting station. She thought about tunnel six.

She had walked past the north wall of tunnel six approximately twelve thousand times. She had walked past it the way she walked past everything in the mines — present enough to avoid walking into it, absent enough to avoid seeing it. She saw it now through a dead man's eyes, and what she saw was that one section of it had been slowly failing for years, and that one push in exactly the right place would free the whole panel, and that nobody had noticed because noticing things in the mines was dangerous and so nobody practiced it.

She took a crystal from the reserve bins. Small, green, unremarkable. Pressed it against her ribs.

She went to tunnel six.

She pressed her palm against the wall.

She pushed.

The stone came free. Behind it was a passage and air that moved, which was not something air in the mines did, and she went through it and upward, using the dead mason's knowledge of stone to navigate the dark, and came out on a hillside under a pale sky.

The sky was larger than she had expected. This was either awe-inspiring or alarming; she defaulted to alarming, because alarming things required plans and awe-inspiring things required time she didn't have.

Something was falling from it. Small. White-gray. It landed on her arm and dissolved.

She had no idea what that was.

She had one crystal, no food, no map, and the Harvesters would find the broken panel by dark. She had made a series of decisions today that could generously be described as *improvised* and less generously as *desperate* and most accurately as *what seemed obvious at the time,* and now she was standing on a hillside in the outside world for the first time in her life and she still had no idea what she was doing.

She started walking.

She was probably going to be terrible at this.
