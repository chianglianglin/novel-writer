# Robin Hobb Draft — Chapter 2

**Confidence:** 89%

**Style Notes:** First-person retrospective throughout; narrator looks back with full knowledge; Senna's joy is the emotional heart — first experience of happiness from the inside, then losing it; Varo rendered with Hobb's psychological precision; the "new cage" observation named plainly; closing gives emotional truth without sentimentalizing.

I have tried to remember, in the years since, what it felt like to be that tired. I find I cannot, which is perhaps a mercy. What I remember instead are the specific details: the way the ash settled on my eyelashes and made the world look blurred. The way the ground sloped in directions I couldn't predict, so that my body kept making adjustments that had nothing to correct against. The weight of the crystal in my pocket, pressing against my ribs as I walked, waiting.

I had been walking for half a day when I heard Varo moving behind me.

I want to say I heard him because I was perceptive. The truth is that he allowed me to hear him — he told me this himself, later. He had been watching the mine exits for five months. He had learned, in that time, that the people who emerged from the mines were in no condition to have strangers materialize silently beside them. He made noise on purpose so that I would have a moment to understand I was not alone before I had to deal with what that meant.

I turned. He was perhaps thirty, with the particular build of someone who had once been mine-thin and had since eaten enough to add mass without ever quite losing the careful way he moved, as if he still expected the tunnel ceiling. He was watching my hands.

I put them behind my back. A reflex, which told me more about myself than I had known.

"Both of them," he said. Not accusatory. Just precise. "Not one. That's new."

I did not know what he meant yet. I know now that in his five months of watching, he had found four other escapees. Three had been recaptured. One — a man named Coll — had survived. None of them had aged hands. He had not known, until he saw me, that the crystals could do that.

He asked me what I had absorbed.

I told him. He listened in the way of someone assembling information rather than responding to it, his eyes going slightly unfocused in the manner of people who are organizing a great deal at once. He asked me how long I had held the skill. I said it was gone within minutes. He said: *I thought so.* He said: *He doesn't know they can be absorbed. He thinks they only disperse.*

*He.* Not *them.* Not *the Harvesters.*

"Who is he?" I asked, though some part of me had always known the shape of the answer.

Varo looked at me the way people look at someone who has said something so obvious it is almost painful. "Morthael," he said. The word landed the way words do when they have never been spoken in your presence before, when you have known them only as a shape around a silence. "The Collector. The god who doesn't die." He paused. "The thing we're going to end."

I had heard the word before. Of course I had heard it. But in the mines, it was never completed. It existed as an intake of breath, a stillness, a direction no one turned their face toward. Hearing it spoken aloud and finished, in the open air, by a man who said it the way you say *wound* when you are pressing on it to check whether it still hurts — that was different. That was a door opening that I had not known was closed.

He said we should move. He said the Harvesters would have found the broken wall by now — perhaps already had.

I took out the crystal.

He stopped walking. He watched me hold it.

"You're going to absorb it," he said.

"I want to know what's in it before you tell me what to do with it," I said. This was not entirely true. I also wanted, for reasons I was not prepared to examine, to do it while I still had the choice about whether to do it at all.

He said nothing. He watched.

I will try to describe what deliberate absorption is like, as opposed to the accident in tunnel four. Deliberately, it is slower. You feel it approach rather than simply finding yourself inside it. There is a moment — perhaps two seconds — where you are both yourself and not-yourself simultaneously, where you can feel the edges of another person's life pressing against the edges of your own. And then you are them, entirely.

Her name was Senna. She had been twenty-three when she died, which was three years before I was born, and she had died in the way of people who do risky work and know the risk and do it anyway, which is to say she had died quickly, without warning, by the very particular bad luck that distinguishes itself from stupidity only in retrospect.

Senna had been a lockpick and a forger. She had been very good at both. She had been, more than any of this, happy — in the specific, unglamorous way of people who have found work they love and people who love them back and have not yet lost either. She had friends who still made her laugh after years of knowing her. She had a particular method of setting a wire into a tumbler mechanism that was elegant in the way only things that have been practiced ten thousand times can be elegant, which is to say it felt inevitable, as if the wire had always been going to go exactly there.

I had her skill and I had her happiness and I had four seconds of knowing what it was like to be a person who was *glad to exist*.

Then she was gone.

I stood on the slope with Varo watching me and my hands showing the new lines around my knuckles — not much, less than the first time, but measurable — and I had to decide whether I was going to weep for a woman I had never met who had been dead for nineteen years, whose joy I had held in my chest for four seconds and then lost.

I decided I was not. This was, I think, the first significant decision I made as a free person, and like most first decisions made by people who have only just become free, it was probably wrong.

"Lockpick," I said. "She was a lockpick."

Varo allowed himself one small expression that was not quite a smile. "That's useful," he said.

"And a forger."

"That's very useful."

He began moving again. I followed. The ash was heavier in the valley below, and the light was going. He moved through the landscape with the ease of someone who had been learning it for years, and I moved behind him like a child following a parent through a crowd, which was a strange thing to feel at sixteen when you had never had parents, which is to say — strange is not quite the right word.

We went underground again.

The passage was different from the mines. Not much, at first — stone walls, low ceiling, the particular silence of below-ground — but different in a way that took me a moment to identify. Then I understood: it had been chosen. Every cut, every turn, every brace and support had been made by people who had decided to be here rather than people who were born into it and had never been offered the alternative.

The safe house was a room carved from natural stone, lit by covered lamps, containing six people who looked up when we came in with the specific attention of people who have been waiting for something that matters. Someone handed me food. I ate it without tasting it. Someone asked Varo a question I didn't hear, and Varo answered, and I caught, in the answer, the word *absorber*, spoken in a way that was not quite reverent but was adjacent to it, the way people speak of things they have theorized for years and are only now seeing confirmed.

I looked at my hands.

Still older than my face.

I had walked out of one kind of dark into another, and the lamps in this one were warmer, and the people in this one had names for their enemy, which was more than the people in my dark had ever managed. But the specific attention in the eyes of everyone in the room — the weight of their wanting, which was already visible before they had spoken a word to me directly — was its own kind of stone.

I had been useful in the mines because I was a body. I was, I could see, about to be useful here because I was a weapon. The bars of this cage were different. They were made of hope and necessity and the specific fragility of a cause that has been waiting a long time for the piece that would make it possible.

I had always been good at noticing bars.

I had not, until that moment, understood how much harder it was to want to leave when the people on the other side were not your enemies.

I ate my food. I did not tell them what I had decided, because I had not decided anything.

That was, if I am honest, the most important fact about that night.
