# Ernest Hemingway Draft

**Confidence:** 80%

**Style Notes:** Iceberg throughout — Morthael unnamed, the death system implied through foreman/Harvesters behavior. Short declarative sentences dominate. All emotion carried by physical specificity. Aldric delivered as factual inventory, not wonder. Falling snow described through sensation without naming it. No adverbs. Every sentence earns its place.

---

She carried the crate the way she always carried it, with her knees bent and her back straight. The crystal inside was wrapped in ash-cloth. She could feel the weight shift when she moved too fast.

The tunnel was thirty feet wide and low enough that tall men walked with their heads down. She was not tall. She had never been tall enough to hit the ceiling. She had been in the mines since she was four, or maybe five. She did not know exactly.

The woman ahead of her dropped her crate.

She heard it before she saw it. A sound like a tooth breaking. Then everything stopped.

The woman stood over the shattered crystal. The men nearby had already stepped back. The foreman came through from the sorting hall. He did not run. He walked the way men walked when they knew they were in charge of what happened next.

She set her own crate down carefully. She looked at the floor.

The crystal had cracked along its center. The light inside it had gone blue-white and wrong. She had seen this twice before. Each time, afterward, she had not seen the person who broke it.

The foreman said something low. Two Harvesters came from the gate.

Then the crystal's light touched her bare hand where she had braced herself against the floor. It lasted maybe two seconds. Maybe less.

Forty-three years came into her at once.

She knew how to cut stone. She knew the difference between limestone and shale by smell. She knew the way stress moved through a wall — how load-bearing stone felt different under the heel of a hand than infill. She knew a man named Aldric, who had a wife and two sons and a bad knee that swelled in winter and a way of singing under his breath when he set the keystone. She knew his whole life. It was complete and it was hers and then it was gone.

She pulled her hand back. She pressed it against her thigh.

Nobody had seen.

The Harvesters were taking the woman through the gate. The other workers had their eyes down. The foreman was writing in his ledger.

She looked at her hand. The skin across her knuckles looked older than she remembered it. Like paper that had been wet and dried. She turned it over. The lines in her palm were deeper.

She did not think about this. She picked up her crate and walked to the sorting hall.

She thought about the wall on the north side of tunnel six. She had walked past it twice a day for eight years. She had never looked at it the way Aldric would have looked at it. She looked at it now, from memory, from his memory, and she knew exactly where the stone was wrong.

She set her crate down at the sorting station. The clerk did not look up.

She walked back. At the reserve bin she took one crystal — small, pale green. She put it in her shirt pocket, against her ribs.

The north wall of tunnel six was forty feet in. She pressed her palm flat against it at waist height, just left of the third iron torch bracket.

The stone gave under pressure. Not much. Enough.

She hit it with her elbow once. The whole panel came loose cleanly, like a tooth that had been ready. Behind it was darkness and a smell she did not recognize.

She went through.

She moved upward for a long time. The passage was natural, not cut. She used her hands on the walls.

She came out on a hillside.

She stood in the open for the first time she could remember and the sky was the color of old bone and something was falling out of it. Small and slow. It landed on her arm. It was lighter than ash. It dissolved when it touched her.

She did not know the word for it. She had heard women talk about water that fell from the sky. She had not known it felt like this. Quiet and cold and soft.

She looked behind her. The hillside was empty.

There was nothing she recognized in any direction. She had one crystal and no food and no idea of the country. The Harvesters would find the broken wall by evening.

She started walking. She walked the way she had always walked — knees bent, back straight.
