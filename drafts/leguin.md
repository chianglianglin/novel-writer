# Ursula K. Le Guin Draft

**Style Notes:** Anthropological precision applied to mines' social conventions; absorption rendered as translation between languages; quiet moral philosophy in Aldric's characterization; measured unhurried rhythm; sky-learning treated as patient inquiry; balance and acceptance in closing movement.

In the mines, the sorting work was done by children and those who had been children in the mines long enough to become adults without knowing anything else. The distinction mattered to no one in authority and had therefore ceased to matter, in most practical senses, to anyone at all.

She had stopped being a child somewhere around her twelfth year, not because anything particular had happened but because the mines did not accommodate childhood beyond a certain point and so she had simply grown out of it, the way one grows out of a garment that no longer fits. What remained of childhood was an aptitude for noticing things that adults had learned to stop noticing, and a habit of wondering about the sky, which she had never seen.

She was wondering about the sky — it was a recurring occupation, like checking the tension of a rope — when the woman named Netta let her crate fall.

The breaking of a crystal was understood in the mines as a social event of a particular kind. It was not discussed. It was not prevented from happening once it had begun. It was witnessed in a specific mode — eyes lowered, attention shifted inward, presence declared as inconsequential — and then it ended, and work resumed. The social function of this witnessing mode was to make visible the line between those who were implicated in an incident and those who were not. She had practiced this mode since childhood.

She practiced it now, setting her crate down and bracing her hand flat on the floor, and the crystal's released light — which behaved, as always, like light did not ordinarily behave — reached her skin.

What she experienced was not a foreign country, though that is the nearest analogy. It was more like being translated into a language she had never learned, and discovering that the language contained concepts her own lacked, and that some of those concepts fit something she had felt for years without having the word for it.

The man had been called Aldric. He had spent his life understanding how structures distributed weight — how a wall was in constant conversation with everything it touched, how failure propagated through stone not randomly but according to principles that could be known. He had been kind to his sons in the specific way of people who have discovered, after difficulty, that kindness is not a weakness. He had died alone on a road in winter, and the road had been named for a battle that happened afterward, and his name was on no monument.

She knew all of this. She knew it for four seconds, and then she did not.

She looked at her hands.

The skin had changed. Not dramatically. Not violently. It had simply moved further along a process that ordinarily took decades, and she could see this clearly because she had made a habit of noticing things. She did not know what it meant. She noted it the way one notes a new fact about the behavior of stone or water — with interest, and without immediate interpretation, because interpretation required more data.

She had, however, enough data about one thing: the north wall of tunnel six.

She had walked past it twelve thousand times. She had not looked at it the way Aldric looked at walls — not as surfaces but as arguments, not as boundaries but as ongoing negotiations between weight and form. She looked at it now with his understanding, which was already beginning to thin and drift at the edges, and she found the place where the argument had been going wrong for sixteen years. One push, deliberate and precisely placed, would resolve it.

She deposited her crate. She took a crystal from the reserve bins — small, pale, unremarkable — and tucked it against her body. She went to tunnel six.

She pushed the wall.

The stone moved. Behind it, air moved.

She climbed the passage in the dark, and the passage was narrow and irregular and required patience, which she had developed over years of work that required patience. She did not rush. She used her hands on the walls. She went upward.

She emerged on a hillside.

The sky was there. It was not what she had imagined, not because it was smaller but because imagination, even practiced and disciplined, cannot account for the actual weight of the world's openness when you encounter it for the first time. The sky was enormous and gray and something was falling from it — slowly, in no hurry, in the way that things fall when they are not afraid of landing.

She held out her hand. The falling things dissolved when they touched her. She held out her hand again. Cold and soft and gone.

She stood on the hillside for a time, learning the sky. Then she considered her situation. It was not favorable. One crystal, no food, no knowledge of the country, a broken wall that would be discovered. It was also the only situation she had, and so she assessed it as accurately as she could and began to walk into it.

She did not know what the falling things were called. She had seen, in the few books available in the mines, a word for water that fell from the sky, but she had not expected it to feel like this — patient, and small, and specifically cold, and dissolving into her as though it had always meant to.

She walked. The sky continued, vast and overcast, and did not end.
