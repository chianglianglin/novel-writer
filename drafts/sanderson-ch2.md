# Brandon Sanderson Draft — Chapter 2

**Confidence:** 87%

**Style Notes:** Third-person limited throughout; Sanderson's First Law honored — deliberate absorption reveals all mechanics BEFORE the skill solves a problem; "Not X. Y." denial structure; italicized internal thoughts; Morthael named as deliberate tool; single-sentence paragraphs; chapter closes on choice suspended.

She'd been walking for six hours when she realized she had no idea what she was looking for.

*Storms.*

The sky wasn't helping. It was the same bone-pale it had been when she surfaced, the ash falling at the same patient rate, and the landscape offered nothing she could read. No paths she recognized. No markers. No tunnels.

She'd learned to navigate underground by counting turns. Up here there were no turns, only slope and wind-direction and the distant shapes of things she had no names for. Hills, maybe. Or they were something else. The dead mason's geography had faded completely by midmorning.

The crystal was still in her pocket.

She'd been thinking about it for six hours. Or not thinking about it, which was the same thing, the way not looking at something in a dark room is still a relationship with it.

She knew what was in it. Or she'd know, for four seconds, as soon as she touched it with her skin and made the choice. The question was whether to make the choice alone on a hillside where she had no idea what skill she might need, or whether to wait until she needed something specific and then find out she'd been carrying a crystal containing, say, the memories of someone who had been very good at embroidery.

She was still arguing with herself about this when Varo stepped out from behind a rock.

He was perhaps thirty. Mine-built — she recognized the posture, the way his shoulders angled inward by reflex, the careful economic quality of his movements. He'd been somewhere like the mines. She didn't know if he'd been in *the* mines.

He was looking at her hands.

Not her face. Her hands.

She curled her fingers. Too late.

"Both of them," he said. Flat, informational. "Both hands. Not just one."

He knew what it meant. She didn't know how he knew, but she could see that he did — the way he was cataloguing her, the slight recalibration happening behind his eyes.

"You're from the fourth shaft," he said. Not a question. "There was a break yesterday."

"There was a break," she said.

"And you touched it."

She didn't say anything.

"You absorbed it." He said it like a theorem being confirmed. "Both hands means you absorbed twice. Once involuntarily and once" — his eyes dropped to her shirt pocket, where the outline of the crystal pressed against the fabric — "deliberately."

*He can see it.*

"Not yet," she said. "I haven't used that one yet."

Something shifted in his assessment of her. She wasn't sure what — whether it went up or down. "You should," he said. "The Harvesters will search this entire slope by nightfall. Whatever's in that crystal, you'll want to know now."

"Who are you?"

"Someone who's been watching that exit for five months." He said it without apology, as if surveillance was a perfectly normal thing to do. Which, she supposed, it was, depending on your circumstances. "Someone who's been waiting for a specific kind of escapee." He paused. "You're the first one."

She had the crystal out before she'd consciously decided to take it out.

He didn't reach for it. Didn't step back. He just watched, and she understood he had done this before — watched someone absorb — which meant he had seen it work, which meant there were others. Somewhere. Who could do what she could do.

*Not X. Not alone in this.*

She pressed the crystal flat against her palm.

The difference between involuntary and deliberate was the difference between a door blowing open and choosing to open it. Deliberate was slower. She felt the edges of the crystal's light before it entered her — felt herself on one side of a threshold and the crystal's content on the other, and then she stepped through.

Senna. Twenty-three. A lockpick and a forger, primarily, but the skill that landed first was the forging — the specific muscle knowledge of making a hand that was not yours look like yours, of understanding documents the way a mason understood walls, as objects with particular internal logic that could be read and replicated and improved upon. She knew which stamps were used in the lower districts. She knew which paper stock the collectors used for transit permits. She knew how to make herself invisible on paper, which was, she had always maintained, at least as important as being invisible in person.

Senna had loved her work. This was the part that came in alongside the skill and could not be separated from it — the texture of her satisfaction, the particular warmth of a door opening under her wire, the sound of a lock resolving. She had been glad when she was working. She had been twenty-three years old and glad, almost every day, which was something so foreign to the protagonist's experience that for four seconds she was not sure she believed it.

Then the crystal went dark.

She exhaled. Her hands — she checked — showed new lines at the joints. Less than before. Still real.

"Forger," she said. "Lockpick."

Varo's expression didn't change, but something in it shifted toward useful. "And the cost?"

She held up her hands. He looked at them for a long moment.

"Each absorption costs you something," he said.

"Yes."

"And the bigger the absorption, the bigger the cost."

She didn't know that. But it tracked. "Probably," she said.

He nodded slowly, the kind of nod that meant he was filing everything into a framework he'd been building for a long time and was now being confirmed piece by piece. "Morthael," he said, abruptly. As if the name itself were a tool he was picking up. "Do you know that name?"

The name hit her in the way Varo had probably intended — as a word that had weight because she had spent her whole life navigating around the space where it should have been.

"In the mines," she said carefully, "no one says it."

"Up here," he said, "we say it constantly. We say it until it stops being a fear and starts being a target." He turned. "Come with me. We're losing light."

She followed him, because the alternative was the hillside alone in the dark with Harvesters somewhere below. She told herself that was the only reason.

She was mostly right.

---

The safe house was an hour's walk down and in — a cut passage that branched off a natural cave, reinforced with lumber in a way that looked planned. Chosen. The kind of construction that came from people building toward something rather than away from it.

Six people were inside. They all looked up when she entered, and the quality of their looking was specific — not curiosity, not threat, but the particular concentrated attention of people whose cause has just acquired something they didn't have before.

Someone handed her food. She ate it.

Varo was speaking to the group in a low voice. She caught, in what he was saying, the shape of what she was about to become to them.

*Absorber.* He used the word twice.

She looked at her hands. The ash had settled into the new lines at her knuckles, tracing them grey.

She had been in the mines because she had no other option. She was in this room because she had no other option. At some point, she was going to be asked to stay for a reason that was not *no other option* — was going to be asked to choose, which was a thing she had never been offered before and did not know how to do.

She wasn't there yet.

She ate her food and looked at her hands and waited to be asked.
