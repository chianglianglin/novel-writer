# Gabriel García Márquez Draft

She had been carrying crystals for so many years that the weight of them had become the weight of her own body, and she could no longer remember what it felt like to be without it. The women who had taught her to sort the pale ones from the green ones had themselves been taught by women who were dead, and those women had been taught by others whose names were lost, and so the knowledge passed downward in an unbroken chain that no one had ever thought to question, because the alternative to not questioning was worse than the question itself.

The mines had existed before Morthael and would exist, she suspected, after him. The stone did not care about gods. The stone had been here when there were no gods, and it would be here again. It was only the crystals that were new — new in the way that a thousand years made something new, which was not very new at all.

The woman in front of her let go of her crate for reasons that would never be fully explained.

The sound of the crystal breaking passed through the tunnel the way a rumor passes through a village: everyone heard it and no one acknowledged having heard it. The light inside the shattered crystal did something terrible and beautiful, bleeding out across the floor in a color that had no name in the language spoken in the mines, and she had just enough time to register that this was the third breaking she had witnessed in her life — the first at age seven, the second at age eleven, and the third now, at what she calculated to be sixteen or seventeen — before her hand, still pressed flat against the floor from when she had set down her crate, felt the light pass into her skin.

For four seconds she lived a life that was not hers and loved people she had never met.

He was a man who had built things, who had woken every morning to the smell of his wife's hair and the sound of his sons' feet on the stone floor, who had pressed his palm against a hundred walls in his life and known each one by its particular conversation with weight and time. His name was Aldric Vann and he had died on a road in winter and the road had been named for someone else. She knew all of this. She knew the color of the river he had bridged and the name of the song he had sung when the work was going well and the particular way his bad knee ached before rain. She knew forty-three years of a man's life with the intimacy of having lived them, and then she was herself again, kneeling on the tunnel floor.

This was the first miracle she had ever experienced and she had no language for it, so she did not think about it in terms of miracles. She thought about it the way she thought about everything in the mines: as a fact that would either kill her or be useful.

She noticed her hands. The skin at her knuckles had aged; she could see this the way you see something you already knew without knowing you knew it. She looked at her hands for three seconds and then she stopped looking at her hands, because there was a wall she needed to visit.

In tunnel six, which she had walked ten thousand times without seeing it the way it wished to be seen, the north wall spoke to her in the language of a dead mason's hands. The stone beside the third bracket had been lying about its weight for sixteen years. Under the flat of her palm she felt the lie as clearly as she had ever felt anything — clearer, in fact, because this was not her knowledge but Aldric's, and Aldric had never been wrong about stone.

She had already collected the crystal from the reserve bins. Pale green, the size of two fingers. She pressed it against her ribs as she pressed her other hand against the wall and pushed.

The stone came out of the wall the way a truth comes out of a person who has been holding it too long. Behind it was a passage that smelled of the world above, and she climbed it through the dark with her hands on the walls, going upward toward the air she had always imagined and never been permitted.

She emerged onto a hillside in the early evening, under a sky the color of ash and bone. For the first time in her life she saw the horizon — the place where the world ended and the sky began — and it was farther than she had believed possible.

Something was falling from the sky. It fell slowly, in spirals, and landed on her skin and dissolved there without heat. She stood with her arms out and caught what fell and let it melt and stood there for a long time doing this, because she had nowhere particular to be and no name yet for what was happening to her.

Somewhere below her, in the tunnels, the Harvesters would find the broken wall before morning. She had one crystal in her shirt and a dead man's knowledge fading from her hands and all the world laid out before her in the failing light.

She began to walk. The ash fell. The horizon did not get closer, and she had learned, from a man she had never met, that this was simply the way horizons worked: you walked toward them your whole life and they kept their distance, which was not a reason to stop walking.
