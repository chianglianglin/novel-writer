**Style Notes:** Third-person limited throughout. Magic system front-loaded per Sanderson's First Law: reader sees what it does AND the cost before it solves the problem. She had carried crates for so long that the ache in her shoulders had stopped being pain.

It was just *her* now.

The Ashfield mines ran seventeen tunnels deep, and she had worked every one of them since the Harvesters brought her here as a child. She knew the tunnels' particular darkness, the way the crystal-light turned the stone walls blue and then sick-white and then blue again. She knew the weight of ash-cloth wrapping, the sound of the second bell, the exact posture that kept the foreman from noticing you.

*Keep your head down. Move at a steady pace. Don't look at the crystals too long.*

She was good at not looking at the crystals too long.

The crystals were the memories of dead people. Every worker in the mines knew this. The knowing didn't make you feel anything after a while. It was like knowing the stone was old, or that the ash came from somewhere distant. Facts accumulated in the mines the way dust did. After enough time, you stopped seeing them.

She was walking past tunnel six's north wall — for roughly the ten-thousandth time, she estimated — when the woman in front of her dropped her crate.

The crystal broke.

*Storms.*

Not out loud. She kept it internal, swallowed it the way you swallowed everything dangerous in the mines. The broken crystal's light was already doing the thing it wasn't supposed to do, that terrible dispersal from blue-white into something that had no clean color name, spreading across the floor like spilled light from a broken lantern.

She knew what happened next. She'd seen it twice before.

She set her crate down. She braced her hand against the floor.

The light reached her palm. It went *in*.

She had no framework for what followed, because nothing she had ever experienced had prepared her for it. She could feel it the way you felt a Harvester's attention — as pressure, immediate and total. Except instead of fear, it was —

*Knowledge.*

His name was Aldric. He had a wife named Betha, who smelled of pine resin and always burned the first batch of bread. He had two sons and a bad left knee and forty-three years of knowing exactly what stone wanted to do and how to help it or stop it.

She knew how to read a wall. She knew which type of cold meant a hollow behind the stone. She knew that the third bracket section in tunnel six had been bearing weight it was never designed to carry, had been bearing it for years, and would release with one precise push at the correct point.

Then it was gone.

Not X. Not *here's this incredible power you've always had and never known about.*

Gone. Faded. She was herself again, kneeling on the stone floor with a dead man's knowledge evaporating from her hands like heat.

She breathed.

She looked at her hands. The skin across her knuckles was wrong. Not wrong the way dirt was wrong — wrong the way *time* was wrong. Older. Thinner. Like her grandmother's hands, if she'd had a grandmother.

*Filing that away,* she thought. *Later.*

The Harvesters were coming through the gate. She rose, collected her crate, and moved toward the sorting station at the pace of someone who had done this eleven thousand times and had opinions about nothing.

The clerk didn't look up.

She deposited her crate, walked back past the reserve bins, and palmed a small pale-green crystal without breaking stride. Pressed it flat against her ribs, under her shirt.

Tunnel six. North wall. Third bracket.

She pressed her palm against the stone at the exact spot the dead mason had spent forty-three years learning to recognize.

She pushed.

The panel came free in a single clean motion. No noise. Behind it: darkness, cold air, and a smell that made something ancient in her chest go *still*.

Moving air. She'd never felt moving air underground before.

She went through.

The climb took longer than she'd expected. The passage was natural, and natural things had opinions about being climbed. She scraped her knees twice. She lost the crystal-glow entirely and navigated by touch, using the mason's knowledge of how rock felt when it was safe to trust.

She emerged on a hillside.

The sky was the first thing she couldn't understand.

She'd seen darkness. She'd seen crystal-glow. She had no category for this — this enormous pale-gray void above her with something falling from it, soft and slow, landing on her outstretched arm and dissolving there cold and perfect and *silent*.

She stood with her arms out.

*Storm it,* she thought, *that's the whole sky. That's just — all of it.*

She had one intact crystal in her pocket. She had a broken wall behind her that the Harvesters would find by nightfall. She had borrowed knowledge fading from her hands second by second, leaving less of Aldric with each heartbeat.

And she had no idea where to go.

But she was *out*.

She started walking. The sky kept falling, soft and cold, and she didn't have a name for it, and she didn't need one yet.

*She'd figure out the names later.*
