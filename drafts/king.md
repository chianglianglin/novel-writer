Here's the thing about the memory mines that nobody tells you, because nobody who's been in the memory mines long enough to tell you anything has ever gotten out: the worst part isn't the darkness or the weight or the crystals themselves, which glow with a light that's somehow worse than no light at all.

The worst part is the *routine* of it.

Up at the fourth bell. Ash-cloth wrapping. Load the crates. Walk the line. Sort. Don't look at the crystals too long. Don't drop anything. *Don't drop anything.* Load the sleds. Ride the second bell down to the lower tunnel. Do it again. The Harvesters come at shift-end with their tallies, and as long as your number is right, you go back to your bunk. You eat whatever they give you.

She'd been doing this since she was four or five — she didn't know exactly, same way she didn't know her last name or what month she'd been born or what rain felt like, which was a thing she'd heard about from the older women. *Water that falls from the sky,* Maret had told her once, *cold and clean and it makes everything smell like the world just woke up.* She'd tried to imagine that. She'd been trying for about twelve years.

She was still trying when Danna dropped her crate.

The sound was — okay. The sound was very specific. You know how a wine glass sounds when you flick it wrong? Now imagine that sound coming from something that used to be a person. A whole person, all their remembering, everything they ever loved or hated or wanted, and now it's just a noise and some light going wrong-colored on the tunnel floor.

She'd heard that sound twice before. Both times, after, the person who'd caused it was just *gone.*

The foreman looked up from his ledger. The way he looked up was already a verdict.

Two Harvesters came through the gate.

She set her crate down — *carefully, carefully* — and knelt as if she was checking the ash-cloth wrapping, bracing one hand against the floor. And that's when the broken crystal's light, that sick blue-white wrong light, reached her bare skin.

*Oh.*

It didn't hurt. That's the thing she kept thinking afterward. She'd expected something to hurt. Instead it was like — have you ever been standing outside on a cold night, and someone opens a door behind you, and for one second you feel the warmth of the whole room on your back? It was like that, except the room was a man's entire life.

His name was Aldric. He had a bad knee. He loved his wife in a way that was embarrassingly specific — the way she tucked her thumb under her palm when she slept, the sound she made at the kettle in the mornings. He'd built things. Stone things. He knew how stone *worked*, not just its surface but its inside, the way it held weight, the way it wanted to come apart. He'd kissed his sons' heads on the last morning he'd had sons to kiss, not knowing. He'd been afraid at the end.

For four seconds she had been Aldric.

Then she was herself again, kneeling on the tunnel floor with her hand against the stone, and the Harvesters were taking Danna through the gate, and Danna was making sounds that she would not describe.

Nobody had seen.

She stood up slowly. She felt like herself. She felt *more* than herself. And she felt — she looked at her hands, and she'd have said she was wrong except she wasn't, because she'd looked at her hands every day for as long as she could remember and she *knew* them. The skin over her knuckles was looser than it should have been. The little lines at her wrist were deeper.

*What did it cost me,* she thought, *and is that all?*

She filed it. She picked up her crate.

Here's the other thing about the memory mines: she'd been walking tunnel six for eight years, twice a day minimum. She'd looked at that north wall about six thousand times without actually *seeing* it. She saw it now with Aldric's eyes, and what she saw was that the third bracket section was a lie. A friendly lie, the kind stone tells when it's been quietly failing for years and nobody's thought to ask it the right question.

She knew the right question. She'd just borrowed it from a dead man.

She made her drop at the sorting station — the clerk never looked up, and why would he, she'd delivered ten thousand crates without incident — and on the way back she dipped into the reserve bin and slipped a pale green crystal into her shirt pocket. Against her ribs, where the Harvesters wouldn't see it unless they stripped her.

She went to tunnel six.

She pressed her palms against the wall where Aldric would have pressed them. She felt the lie in the stone.

She pushed.

The panel came out of the wall like a held breath releasing. Behind it: darkness, cold air, and a smell she'd never smelled before. *Moving* air. She didn't have a word for it and she didn't need one.

She went in. She went up. She went until the stone gave way to earth and the earth gave way to open hillside and the sky opened above her like something enormous deciding, finally, to stop pretending to have a ceiling.

She stood in the outside world and tried to understand it.

The sky was the color of ash — which she knew, because the mines were full of ash, and it was nothing like she'd imagined. Something was falling from it. Small. Slow. It landed on her arm and she pulled up her sleeve and watched it dissolve against her skin, cold and delicate and nothing like ash at all.

*So that's what that is,* she thought, and didn't have a word for that either.

The Harvesters would find the broken panel by nightfall. She had one crystal in her pocket and no food and no idea of the country. She had a dead man's knowledge in her hands, which were already forgetting it, the way you forget a dream you don't write down.

She started walking.

The world was very large. She had always suspected this, and it turned out she was right.
