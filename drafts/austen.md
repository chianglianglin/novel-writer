# Jane Austen Draft

**Confidence:** 81%

**Style Notes:** Free indirect discourse sustained throughout; the ironic drawing-room register applied to the mine's brutal social logic; the absorption delivered with Austen's characteristic dry understatement; structural weakness treated as social analysis; closing maintains ironic composure while the situation is genuinely dire.

It is a truth not universally acknowledged — for there are very few persons in the memory mines in a position to acknowledge truths of any kind — that the surest way to survive the crystal sorting lines is to become, to the greatest possible extent, invisible.

She had achieved this. Indeed, she had achieved it so thoroughly that she had sometimes wondered whether she existed at all, in any meaningful sense, or whether she was merely a function of the sorting line, a mechanism for moving crates from one end of a tunnel to another, occasionally fed and rested so as to continue performing this service.

This was not, she reflected, an entirely unpleasant existence. It was simply a very small one.

She was considering this — the smallness of her existence, which she had never previously considered worth examining and which presented itself to her now with unusual urgency — when the woman in front of her dropped her crate.

The crystal inside broke with a sound that carried through the tunnel in an extremely definitive manner.

Now, the conventions of the sorting lines were, in their way, as strict as any society's codes. One did not run. One did not exclaim. One looked at the floor. One allowed those in authority — the foreman, and above the foreman, the Harvesters, and above the Harvesters, a name one did not speak — to conduct their business without impediment. This was understood.

She performed all the correct social responses with the practiced ease of long habit. She set down her crate. She looked at the floor. She braced her hand against the stone.

The broken crystal's light reached her palm.

What followed was, to put it plainly, remarkable. She found herself in possession, without warning or invitation, of the entire interior life of one Aldric Vann, master stonemason, deceased. She knew his professional opinions on load-bearing architecture, which were extensive and deeply felt. She knew his domestic arrangements, which had been, by all evidence, a source of genuine and uncomplicated happiness. She knew the precise location of a structural weakness in the north wall of tunnel six, which he would have noticed within approximately thirty seconds of passing it and which had evidently escaped the attention of every Harvester, overseer, and mine engineer for the past sixteen years.

The experience lasted four seconds. It then concluded.

She found herself once more in possession only of her own interior, which felt, by comparison, rather sparsely furnished.

She rose and collected her crate. The Harvesters were attending to the woman who had dropped her crate, which precluded any attention being paid to herself — a social situation she recognized, and took advantage of, with what she hoped was appropriate discretion.

Her hands, she noticed, appeared older. The skin at her knuckles had a papery quality that had not been there this morning. She filed this observation alongside everything else she had recently acquired.

The sorting station clerk did not look up when she deposited her crate. This was his consistent practice, and she had always considered it one of his finer qualities.

She visited the reserve bins. She selected a small pale crystal, unremarkable in every way, and placed it in her pocket. She had developed, over years, a useful theory regarding the reserve bins: since the count was taken at shift-end and the bins were refilled each morning from the upper processing level, a single crystal removed at mid-shift would not be missed until well after she — or whoever had taken it — was no longer present to be asked about it.

She went to tunnel six.

The north wall presented itself to her critical inspection exactly as Aldric had predicted it would. The stone at the third bracket was subtly different from its neighbors — it gave slightly under the flat of her palm, the cold of it fractionally less than the stone on either side, which was precisely the wrong sort of cold for load-bearing material.

She pushed. The panel came free. Behind it was a passage.

She went through.

The climb was long and the passage was not designed for climbing, being a natural fault in the rock, but it was navigable for someone of moderate determination and a thorough, newly acquired understanding of how stone wished to be treated.

She emerged onto a hillside.

The sky was vast. She had expected it to be vast — it was, she understood, one of the sky's defining characteristics — but expectation and experience proved, as so often, to be quite different things. The sky was the color of old linen and something was falling from it.

She caught a flake on her outstretched palm. It dissolved.

She caught another. Same.

She stood on the hillside with her borrowed knowledge already beginning to fade at the edges, like a conversation recalled the morning after, and considered her position. The position was, by any objective measure, rather exposed. One intact crystal. Aging hands whose implications she had not yet finished considering. A broken wall that would be noticed by nightfall. No provisions, no direction, no connections in the outside world.

It was, she thought, a beginning. It was not, perhaps, the beginning she would have chosen had anyone consulted her on the matter.

She straightened her spine, which was a habit she had maintained through twelve years in the mines and saw no reason to abandon now, and began to walk.
