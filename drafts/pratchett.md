# Terry Pratchett Draft

**Confidence:** 85%

**Style Notes:** Multi-level satire throughout; six footnotes with the best jokes (footnote 14th amendment is satirical heart; footnote 6 is the chapter's genuine human truth); comic personification of the wall; closing is both a joke and a true statement about agency; humanist sincerity earned through comedy.

---

The Ashfield memory mines operated according to a set of regulations¹ that had been amended four hundred and sixteen times in the past thousand years, primarily to address administrative edge cases that arose when the original regulations proved insufficiently specific about what, exactly, constituted a violation.²

She had been working the sorting lines since she was approximately four years old³ and had, in that time, developed a sophisticated understanding of which regulations mattered and which ones existed primarily to give the foremen something to cite.

The regulation against dropping crystal crates, for instance, definitely mattered.

The woman in front of her dropped a crystal crate.

The resulting sound was the kind that makes everyone in an enclosed space simultaneously decide that they have pressing reasons to be looking at something else. In the memory mines this was accomplished with the ease of long practice. The foreman looked at his ledger. The workers looked at the floor. The Harvesters, who were approaching from the gate, looked at the woman who had dropped the crate with the particular expression of persons who are about to be extremely bureaucratic about it.

She looked at her crate. She set it down. She braced her hand on the floor in the way she had developed over years of bracing her hands on floors whenever anything alarming happened, on the theory that it was difficult to make things worse by touching the floor.

The crystal's light found her palm.

For four seconds she was Aldric Vann, a master stonemason who had spent forty-three years being very good at something nobody in the memory mines had ever thought to be good at, which was understanding walls.

She knew his wife. She knew his sons. She knew the particular satisfaction of setting a keystone and knowing it would hold, and the bridge at the Selt crossing, and the song he sang when the work was going well, and the bread Betha pressed into his hands the last morning, and the cold road, and the dark at the end of it.

Then she was herself again.

She noted that her hands looked older. She filed this under *problems that aren't immediately fatal*, which was a category she maintained with some care.⁴

The Harvesters were doing what Harvesters did when someone dropped a crate, which did not bear describing⁵ and which she had heard about and hoped never to experience and was currently not experiencing because nobody had seen her hand touch the broken crystal's light, largely because everyone was practicing the regulatory art of not seeing things.

She stood up, collected her crate, and thought about tunnel six.

The north wall of tunnel six had been, from a master stonemason's perspective, a scandal for sixteen years. The stone at the third bracket had been making a structural argument it couldn't support, and the only reason nobody had noticed was that nobody who worked in the mines was allowed to know enough to notice, which was, if you thought about it, a rather elegant design flaw in the system.⁶

She took a crystal from the reserve bins. Small. Green. Unremarkable to everyone who did not know that she now had plans for it.

She went to tunnel six. She pressed her palm against the wall at the place only a dead mason would have known to press.

She pushed.

The wall, which had been building up to this for sixteen years, was somewhat relieved about it.

She went through the passage in the dark and climbed upward for what felt like considerably longer than it probably was,⁷ and came out on a hillside under a sky.

The sky was, she had to admit, extremely large. She had known intellectually that it was large — she had not expected to find that *large*, in this context, was doing the same kind of work that *wet* does in the phrase *the ocean is wet.*

Something was falling from it.

She held out her hand. The thing fell onto her palm and dissolved. She held out her hand again. Same. Cold and soft and specifically itself.

She stood on the hillside catching falling sky-things and letting them dissolve and thought about her situation, which was as follows: one crystal, no food, no map, Harvesters who would find the broken wall before dark, and hands that were older than they had been this morning by an unclear but concerning amount.

On the positive side: she was outside. She had not been outside before. It was, setting aside the ash-colored sky and the falling unknown substances and the imminent pursuit, quite something.

She started walking. She didn't know where she was going. She had never known where she was going. But previously she had known exactly where she was, and at least one of those things had improved.
