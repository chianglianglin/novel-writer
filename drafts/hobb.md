# Robin Hobb Draft

**Confidence:** 87%

**Style Notes:** First-person retrospective throughout; narrator looks back with adult understanding; the absorption's emotional core — not being alone for four seconds — lands as the psychological heart; Aldric rendered through intimate domestic detail; cost present but unexplained; emergence honest about fear rather than triumphant; closing subverts the "hero walks toward destiny" trope.

---

I have tried, in the years since, to describe what it felt like to absorb a crystal for the first time. I have failed every attempt. The best I can say is this: it did not feel like receiving. It felt like remembering something I had never learned.

I was sixteen, or perhaps seventeen — the Harvesters kept the records and did not share them with us. I had been in the mines since I was too small to remember anything else. I did not know the texture of grass or the particular silence of a field at night. I knew stone. I knew ash-cloth and crate-weight and the sound of the second bell, and I knew, in the careful way that kept one alive, exactly how to make oneself invisible to the people in charge of one's continuation.

It was the woman called Netta who dropped her crate. I had liked Netta in the quiet way one liked people in the mines — at a distance, without attachment, because attachment in the mines was a liability. She had always walked steadily. I had not thought her the type to drop things.

I knelt as if checking my own crate's wrapping. My hand touched the floor at the same moment the broken crystal's light reached it.

What I can tell you, and have not been able to tell anyone who would understand it: I was not alone, for four seconds, for the first time in my life.

Aldric had a wife. Her name was Betha. On the morning he left for the last time, she had pressed a piece of bread into his hands and told him to eat it before it went cold. He had not eaten it. He had carried it for half a day before it crumbled. He had been ashamed of this, afterward, though there was no one to be ashamed in front of. He had built the bridge at the Selt crossing, which held for sixty years after his death. He had never thought it particularly significant.

I knew all of this, and then I was alone again.

I have tried, also, to describe what that felt like. The returning. I have not succeeded at that either.

I looked at my hands. The skin at my knuckles had gone thin — thinner than it should have been, thinner than a girl of sixteen or seventeen had any cause to have. I pressed my thumb against the back of my hand and watched the skin move slowly back, the way old skin moves.

I did not understand what this meant. I filed it with the other things I did not understand.

I have since learned what it costs. But I did not know then, and I think now that not knowing was — not merciful, exactly. The mines were not a place of mercy. But it was perhaps the correct ignorance for the moment. Knowing the cost in full might have stopped me. And I needed, in that moment, not to be stopped.

The wall in tunnel six: I had walked past it perhaps twelve thousand times. I had looked at it in the way I looked at everything in the mines, which was to say I had registered it as a surface and then moved my attention elsewhere. I looked at it now through Aldric's accumulated knowledge and I felt, with my palm flat against the stone, the particular cold of a lie. The wall was pretending. It had been pretending for years. One push, properly placed, would free the panel.

I took a crystal from the reserve bins. I did not think about this for very long. The thinking would come later, when there was space for it; in the mines, action was always simpler than thought because thought required a future and the mines discouraged the future as a concept.

I pushed the wall. The wall told its last lie and then stopped.

The passage behind it smelled of things I had no names for. I climbed upward through the dark for a time I did not measure.

I came up onto open ground.

I will not pretend that emerging from the mines was the magnificent experience I had, in small underground moments, sometimes imagined. I was frightened. I had not known I was frightened of open space until it presented itself to me in full. The sky was enormous and pale and completely without walls and I stood at the edge of the hillside gripping the stone lip of my exit and did not, for a long moment, let go.

Something was falling from the sky. Soft. Cold. It dissolved on my hands and left nothing.

I do not know how long I stood there. Long enough that the ash — I know now it was not ash, but ash was the word I had — had dusted my shoulders and my hair.

I had one crystal in my pocket. The Harvesters would find the broken wall. I had nowhere to go and no knowledge of how to get there.

I let go of the stone.

I had no map of the world above. I had no experience of it. I had a dead man's fading recollections of rivers and bread and the weight of stone, and I had been alone my whole life, and I was more alone now than I had ever been because at least in the mines I had known the shape of the walls.

I walked anyway.

I want to say, now, that I walked toward something. Toward freedom or purpose or destiny, any of the clean words that make a story out of a girl walking across a hillside in the falling ash because she had no other option.

I walked away from something. That is the truer telling.
